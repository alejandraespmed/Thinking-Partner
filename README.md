## If I was required to craft a hypothetical case-study based around a challenge faced by an organisation what would be a good scenario to showcase the many idiosyncrasies or facets of decision making

A particularly strong case study would be one where **there is no obviously correct answer**. The organisation should face a decision where different stakeholders have legitimate interests, the information is incomplete, the consequences unfold over different time horizons, and the decision-maker has to choose what to prioritise.
A scenario I'd recommend: **The troubled transformation programme**
Imagine a mid-sized organisation—say, a **national logistics company**—that has spent two years and €40m developing a new digital operating platform.
The programme is now in trouble.
The new system is technically functional, but implementation has been delayed, costs are 35% over budget, employees are resistant to it, and several major customers have complained about disruption during pilots.
The executive team has to decide what to do.
They have three broad options:
1.**Continue** — invest another €15m and try to complete the transformation.
2.**Scale back** — retain the parts that work and abandon the more ambitious elements.
3.**Stop** — write off much of the investment and return to the existing system while developing a new strategy.
What makes this interesting is that **the decision isn't really about choosing between three options**. It's about how the organisation reasons its way toward a decision.
The case can expose lots of facets of decision-making

| Facet | How it appears in the case |
| --- | --- |
| **Strategic thinking** | Is the transformation still aligned with the organisation's long-term strategy? |
| **Financial decision-making** | Should sunk costs influence the decision? What is the expected return on further investment? |
| **Risk** | What are the risks of continuing versus stopping? |
| **Uncertainty** | Nobody knows exactly how much further the programme will cost. |
| **Sunk-cost bias** | "We've already spent €40m" becomes a powerful argument. |
| **Groupthink** | Senior leaders may be reluctant to challenge the executive who originally sponsored it. |

You could make it much richer with an unfolding case
Rather than giving students/participants all the information at once, I'd structure it in **stages**.
**Stage 1 — The initial decision**
They receive the executive briefing and financial information.

> *"You are the executive committee. What should the organisation do?"*
> 

They have to make an initial recommendation.
Then introduce...
**Stage 2 — New information**
A major customer announces that it may leave unless the new platform is operational within 12 months.
Now continuing looks more attractive.
But then...
**Stage 3 — Internal information**
An anonymous employee survey reveals that 62% of operational staff don't believe the system is ready.
Now stopping or delaying looks more attractive.
Then...
**Stage 4 — Political pressure**
The board chair reveals that the organisation has publicly committed to the transformation and cancelling it would be embarrassing.
This introduces **reputation and organisational politics**.
Then...
**Stage 5 — The financial shock**
The CFO discovers that the estimated €15m required to finish the programme was based on optimistic assumptions and could actually be €25–30m.
Now participants have to revisit their assumptions.
And finally...
**Stage 6 — The decision**
The CEO asks:

> **"We have to decide tomorrow. What do you recommend, why, and what would have to be true for your recommendation to work?"**
> 

That last question is particularly useful because it forces people beyond simply saying *"Option B"*.
They have to articulate:

- their assumptions;
- what evidence they relied upon;
- what they don't know;
- which risks they're accepting;
- whose interests they're prioritising;
- what would change their mind;
- and how they would implement the decision.

## Is there any latest research in the phycology of decision making that we could incorporate?”

Yes — and there is some **very relevant recent research** that could make the case study much more sophisticated than simply teaching the usual list of cognitive biases.
One particularly useful development is that researchers are increasingly moving from **“people have biases”** toward the more interesting question: **“How does the decision environment cause, amplify, or mitigate those biases?”** A 2024/25 review specifically examined interventions for organisational decision-making and distinguishes between *debiasing* individuals and redesigning the *choice architecture/process* around them. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01492063241287188?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**For your case study, I'd incorporate at least these ideas:**

1. Don't make the participants the only source of bias

The newer literature suggests that you can get interesting effects by changing the **decision architecture**.

For example, give one executive team:

> "Here are the three options. Discuss and decide."
> 

Give another:

> "Before discussing, independently estimate the probability of success for each option. Identify what evidence would change your mind. Then discuss."
> 

Give a third:

> "One person has been assigned to argue the strongest case against the emerging consensus."
> 

You can then explore how **the structure of the decision process changes the decision**, rather than simply asking whether individuals are biased. This maps directly onto recent work distinguishing individual debiasing from choice-architecture interventions. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01492063241287188?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

Give them an opportunity to **change their minds**

I'd make this central to the case.

One of the fascinating questions in contemporary decision research is what happens **after the decision**. Decision-making isn't necessarily finished when someone says "Option B"; people continue monitoring confidence, processing new evidence and sometimes revising their judgement.

That means you could structure the case as:

**Decision 1 → New evidence → Decision 2 → Consequences → Reflection**

Then compare:

> *"What did you decide?"*
> 

with:

> *"How did you respond when the evidence changed?"*
> 

That lets you examine **escalation of commitment, confirmation bias, confidence, learning and adaptability** within one exercise.

## If you could ask any questions and explore every angle of this, what would you be most curious to ask?”

If I were designing this from scratch, I’d actually become less interested in **“what decision should the organisation make?”** and much more interested in **“what is happening inside the decision-making system while people are trying to make it?”**

I’d want to interrogate the case from several levels.

1. The decision itself

- What exactly is the decision we think we're making?
- Is there actually one decision, or a collection of linked decisions?
- What happens if we make no decision?
- Which decisions are reversible?
- Which are effectively irreversible?
- Could we delay the decision and buy ourselves information?
- What would constitute a *good* decision if the eventual outcome is bad?
- Conversely, could a poor decision produce a good outcome through luck?
- Are we evaluating the **quality of the decision process** or the **quality of the outcome**?

That last distinction could be a really powerful theme.

The really interesting question: what if everyone is rational?

I'd construct the case so that every participant can make a perfectly rational argument.

Then ask:

> **How can rational people collectively produce an irrational outcome?**
> 

That's, to me, one of the richest questions in organisational decision-making.

It moves you beyond:

**"Humans have cognitive biases."**

towards:

**"What properties of groups, incentives, information structures and institutions cause certain outcomes to emerge?"**

And finally, I'd turn the lens around

After the case, I'd ask participants:

> **If you could redesign the decision-making process you just experienced, what would you change?**
> 

They might suggest:

- anonymous initial estimates;
- independent analysis;
- devil's advocates;
- pre-mortems;
- probability estimates;
- decision journals;
- structured dissent;
- separating information gathering from decision-making;
- different voting procedures;
- delaying the decision;
- appointing someone to challenge assumptions.

Then I'd ask the killer question:

> 

That creates a fascinating feedback loop.

They're no longer studying **decision-making in organisations**.

They're studying **their own decision-making while simultaneously studying decision-making**.

The question I'd build the entire case around

If I had to reduce all of this to one central question, it would be:

> **When intelligent, well-intentioned people have incomplete information, conflicting incentives, different perspectives, limited time and unequal power, what actually determines the decision they eventually make?**
> 

And then I'd deliberately avoid telling them that the answer is "cognitive bias."

Because it might be:

**bias + incentives + information + identity + power + uncertainty + group dynamics + organisational culture + decision architecture + luck.**

That, I think, could turn your case study into something considerably more ambitious than a conventional management exercise: **a controlled exploration of how human judgement becomes organisational action.**

## What working theories or hypotheses are we entertaining here? List them so I can test each one on its own.

I’d separate these into **hypotheses about individual cognition, group dynamics, organisational structure, and the decision process itself**. That makes them independently testable rather than collapsing everything into “bias.”

Here’s the working hypothesis set I’d use.

| ID | Working hypothesis | Testable prediction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **H1** | **Confidence exceeds calibration.** | Participants' stated confidence will systematically exceed their actual accuracy. |
| **H2** | **People anchor on initial estimates.** | An initial recommendation/probability will disproportionately influence subsequent judgements, even after new evidence. |
| **H3** | **People under-update.** | Participants will revise their beliefs less than the strength of new evidence warrants. |
| **H4** | **People overweight salient information.** | Vivid anecdotes or emotionally striking information will influence decisions more than equally/relevantly informative but abstract evidence. |
| **H5** | **Quantified information receives disproportionate weight.** | Participants will prefer numerical evidence over relevant qualitative evidence, even when the numerical evidence is not necessarily more diagnostic. |
| **H6** | **People exhibit asymmetric loss sensitivity.** | Potential losses will influence choices more strongly than equivalent potential gains. |
| **H7** | **People protect prior commitments.** | Participants will reinterpret ambiguous evidence in ways that preserve their previous decision. |
| **H8** | **People mistake coherence for accuracy.** | A compelling, internally consistent narrative will be judged more favourably than a less coherent but better-supported explanation. |

| --- | --- | --- |

| **H10** | **Confidence can become detached from evidence.** | Confidence may remain high even when the underlying evidence deteriorates. |
| **H11** | **Prompting metacognition improves decisions.** | Asking people to identify assumptions, uncertainty and disconfirming evidence will improve calibration or decision quality. |
| **H12** | **Knowing uncertainty isn't equivalent to acting on it.** | Participants may correctly identify uncertainty while still making decisions as though the uncertainty doesn't exist. |

| --- | --- | --- |

And there are three assumptions I'd challenge rather than accept

This is where I think the project gets genuinely interesting.

Assumption A: **"Bias is the problem."**

Maybe.

But the newer organisational perspective suggests we should also investigate whether **the environment creates predictable errors**. The distinction between debiasing individuals and changing choice architecture is therefore important. That gives us a really clean experimental distinction:

**Decision quality ≠ outcome quality.**

And I'd make participants confront that distinction explicitly.

## One final methodological caution

I would **not describe the case study as "testing whether these psychological theories are true."**

That's too ambitious.

Instead, I'd describe it as:

> **A controlled case-based investigation of how information presentation, individual judgement, group interaction and decision architecture influence decision processes under uncertainty.**
> 

Then each hypothesis becomes an **experimental manipulation**.

For example:

**Same case → different condition**

- quantified vs qualitative information;
- individual decision vs group decision;
- anonymous initial judgement vs public initial judgement;
- structured dissent vs ordinary discussion;
- time pressure vs deliberation;
- decision journal vs no decision journal.

Now you can actually observe **which mechanisms move the decision**, rather than retrospectively explaining every decision with a list of biases.

And importantly, the evidence we've looked at suggests we should retain **medium-confidence and even some low-confidence hypotheses as things to test**, rather than treating the psychological literature as a catalogue of established laws. The hidden-profile literature itself illustrates why: the basic phenomenon is robust, but *why* it occurs and when interventions work is considerably more conditional. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21896790/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That distinction could become one of the strongest intellectual features of the whole case study.

## Could you give me a single deep-research prompt that encompasses everything we covered?

Conduct a comprehensive, critical deep-research investigation into **human and organisational decision-making under uncertainty**, with the ultimate aim of designing a rigorous, research-informed case study or simulation that allows participants to experience, observe and reflect on the mechanisms that shape organisational decisions.

> **When intelligent, well-intentioned people have incomplete information, conflicting objectives, limited time, unequal power and uncertain consequences, what actually determines the decision an organisation eventually makes—and which aspects of the decision environment can improve or degrade that process?**

H9. Confidence is an imperfect indicator of reasoning quality or accuracy.

H11. Metacognitive prompts can improve decision quality, calibration or belief updating.

H12. Recognising uncertainty does not necessarily result in behaviour that appropriately reflects that uncertainty.

Investigate particularly recent research on metacognitive monitoring, confidence, effort allocation, calibration and belief revision.

**Group decision-making**

H13. Groups do not simply aggregate the intelligence or accuracy of their individual members.

H14. First-expressed opinions can anchor subsequent group judgement.

H15. Groups disproportionately discuss information already shared by members rather than unique information held by individual members (the hidden-profile/shared-information problem).

H16. Groups can converge before adequately exploring alternatives.

H17. Structured dissent, devil's advocacy, premortems or equivalent interventions can improve information processing or decision quality.

H18. Confidence, status or perceived expertise can influence group decisions independently of evidential quality.

H19. Group discussion can increase confidence without producing a corresponding increase in accuracy.

Do not merely repeat the classic hidden-profile literature. Investigate newer explanations, moderators and boundary conditions, including whether information-sharing failures are motivational, structural, informational or social.

**Power, identity and incentives**

H20. Hierarchical/status differences suppress the expression of dissent under certain conditions.

H21. Private judgements differ systematically from publicly expressed judgements when social or reputational costs exist.

H22. Ownership of an initiative increases escalation of commitment.

H23. Identity-relevant beliefs are harder to revise in response to contradictory evidence.

H24. Reputational considerations alter decisions that otherwise appear to be purely analytical.

H25. Incentives influence how people interpret ambiguous evidence rather than merely changing their final preferences.

Treat these as conditional hypotheses rather than universal laws.

**Decision architecture**

H26. The structure of the decision process can materially influence outcomes independently of the people making the decision.

H27. Eliciting independent judgements before group discussion reduces anchoring or conformity.

H28. Structured challenge increases identification of risks, alternatives or failure modes.

H29. The sequence in which identical information is presented changes judgement.

H30. Additional information can eventually reduce decision quality through overload, distraction or selective attention.

H31. Time pressure changes not merely the speed but the nature of decision-making.

Investigate recent organisational research distinguishing **individual debiasing interventions** from **choice-architecture/process interventions**.

**Risk and uncertainty**

H32. People respond differently to measurable risk and deeper uncertainty/ambiguity.

H33. Apparently precise numerical estimates can receive more weight than the quality of the underlying evidence warrants.

H34. People assign precise probabilities even when the available evidence does not justify that precision.

H35. Perceived reversibility changes willingness to accept uncertainty and risk.

H36. People systematically underestimate or misuse the value of obtaining additional information before making an important decision.

Be particularly critical of H36 and do not retain it as a core hypothesis unless the evidence supports a sufficiently precise formulation.

**Outcome versus process**

H37. People retrospectively judge decisions partly through the lens of their eventual outcomes.

H38. Lucky outcomes can cause poor decisions to be perceived as good decisions, while unlucky outcomes can cause good decisions to be perceived as poor decisions.

H39. Providing information about what was actually knowable at the time changes retrospective evaluations of decision quality.

H40. Appropriate willingness to revise a decision in response to new evidence is an important component of decision quality.

3. Identify the most defensible core hypotheses

After reviewing all 40 hypotheses, do not treat them equally.

Create three categories:

**Strong evidence**

Claims sufficiently supported to form the foundation of the case.

**Conditional evidence**

Claims worth testing because evidence exists but important boundary conditions remain.

**Exploratory**

Interesting hypotheses that should be presented explicitly as questions rather than established findings.

Explain why each hypothesis belongs in its category.

## Does this update your thinking? Anything unexpected?

Yes. The prompt changes my thinking in a fairly important way.

The biggest shift is that I would **stop treating this primarily as a case study about cognitive biases**. I'd now frame it as a case study about **the production of organisational judgement**.

A few things stand out as unexpectedly important.

1. The "bias" explanation is becoming less satisfying

Our original instinct was:

> People make decisions → psychology introduces biases → teach people about the biases.
> 

The research question is more interesting:

> **People + information + incentives + hierarchy + procedure + uncertainty → organisational decision.**
> 

That means a person can make a perfectly understandable decision and still be part of a systematically poor decision process.

That's a much richer proposition.

2. The decision *architecture* may be as interesting as the psychology

This is probably the biggest update.

Instead of asking:

> "How do we make people less biased?"
> 

I'd ask:

> **"How should we construct the decision environment so that ordinary human psychology produces better decisions?"**
> 

That opens up experimentally testable interventions:

- private judgement before discussion;
- structured dissent;
- explicit uncertainty;
- separating evidence from recommendation;
- forcing alternative hypotheses;
- decision journals;
- probability estimates;
- premortems;
- sequencing information differently.

And critically, some of these may work **without requiring participants to become better thinkers**.

3. "More rational" may not mean "more quantitative"

The quantification finding is particularly interesting.

We intuitively think:

> intuition → subjective → dangerous
> 
> 
> numbers → objective → better
> 

But if people systematically overweight numerical information simply because it is numerical, then **quantification itself becomes part of the psychology of the decision**.

That gives us a beautiful case-study manipulation:

**Same underlying evidence.**

One group receives:

> "Customers are increasingly frustrated and several account managers report declining confidence."
> 

Another receives:

> "Customer confidence has declined by 17%."
> 

Then ask what they do with it.

That is a much subtler demonstration than "here's a classic anchoring bias."

4. I think the case should contain **good reasons to disagree**

This is another important change.

I'd originally imagined people disagreeing because some are biased.

I now think the case should deliberately make disagreement **legitimate**.

The CFO isn't irrational.

The COO isn't irrational.

The CTO isn't irrational.

The customer isn't irrational.

The employee representative isn't irrational.

They have **different objectives, information and tolerances for risk**.

That lets us explore something deeper:

> **How does an organisation aggregate legitimate disagreement when there isn't a single objectively correct objective function?**
> 

That's partly psychology, but it's also governance.

5. I'd make uncertainty itself one of the characters

The original case had uncertainty as background.

I'd now make it central.

Participants should repeatedly encounter situations where they have to say:

> "I don't know."
> 

Then force them to distinguish:

- *I don't know the probability.*
- *I know the probability only approximately.*
- *I don't know which model is appropriate.*
- *I don't know what information would resolve this.*
- *I know what would resolve it, but obtaining it takes six months.*
- *We have to decide anyway.*

That's potentially more valuable than teaching probability theory.

6. The most interesting variable may be **revision**

I increasingly think the case shouldn't primarily test:

> **Who makes the best initial decision?**
> 

It should test:

> **Who changes their mind appropriately when reality changes?**
> 

That allows us to distinguish:

**Consistency**

from

**stubbornness**

and

**flexibility**

from

**indecision**.

You could therefore deliberately give everyone an initially reasonable position and then progressively introduce evidence.

The fascinating measurement becomes:

> **How much should you update—and how much did you actually update?**
> 

7. There's a potentially beautiful "luck" experiment hiding in this

I'd now definitely include stochastic outcomes.

Imagine two teams make decisions.

**Team A:** excellent reasoning, poor outcome because an unlikely event occurs.

**Team B:** poor reasoning, excellent outcome because they get lucky.

Then ask everyone:

> "Which team made the better decision?"
> 

If participants judge Team B more favourably because of the outcome, you've exposed **outcome bias** in a very visceral way.

And then ask:

> "Would you make the same judgement if you only knew what information was available *before* the outcome?"
> 

That could be one of the strongest moments in the case.

8. I wouldn't actually try to test all 40 hypotheses

This is another thing I'd change.

The 40 hypotheses are useful as a **research map**, but trying to cram them all into one case would turn it into a psychology theme park.

I'd select perhaps **6–8 mechanisms** that interact.

Something like:

**Uncertainty**

↓

**Information architecture**

↓

**Individual judgement**

↓

**Group discussion**

↓

**Power/incentives**

↓

**Decision**

↓

**New evidence**

↓

**Revision**

↓

**Outcome**

↓

**Retrospective evaluation**

That gives the case a narrative *and* an experimental structure.

**And there's one question I hadn't appreciated before**

I think the deepest question emerging from all of this is:

> **When should we trust the decision process rather than the decision-maker?**
> 

Imagine someone says:

> "I trust Sarah. She's extremely experienced."
> 

But suppose Sarah's decision process doesn't:

- expose assumptions;
- quantify uncertainty;
- seek disconfirming evidence;
- distinguish knowns from unknowns;
- solicit dissent;
- consider alternatives;
- record what would change her mind.

Should organisational decision-making depend on **the quality of the person**, or can we build processes that make good decisions more likely regardless of who happens to be in the room?

That moves us from psychology into **organisational design**.

And I think that's where this could become much more interesting than a conventional decision-making case study.

**My revised thesis**

I'd now formulate the project approximately as:

> **Good organisational decision-making is not simply a property of rational individuals. It emerges from the interaction between human cognition, information, incentives, social dynamics, uncertainty and decision architecture.**
> 

And the case becomes a way of **letting participants experience that proposition rather than merely being told it**.

That feels like a considerably stronger foundation for the project than where we started.

## "What prompt should I have used from the very start, and what would have been the most useful questions to ask?”

Yes. Looking back over the whole exploration, I think the original prompt was **too solution-oriented too early**. We started by asking, essentially, *“What interesting organisational case could we build?”* The better starting point would have been to ask:

> **What phenomenon are we actually trying to understand, what competing explanations might account for it, and how could we construct an organisational situation that lets us distinguish between them?**
> 

That shift would have saved a lot of subsequent iteration.

**The prompt I would use from the very beginning**

I want to develop a rigorous hypothetical organisational case study that can also function as a research-informed simulation of decision-making under uncertainty.

Do not begin by designing the case. First investigate what the case should actually be designed to study.

The overarching question is:

**When intelligent, well-intentioned people have incomplete information, conflicting objectives, uncertain consequences, limited time, unequal power and social pressures, what actually determines the decision an organisation eventually makes?**

I want to understand the relative contribution and interaction of:

- individual judgement and cognitive biases
- confidence and metacognition
- uncertainty and probabilistic reasoning
- information quality, quantity and sequencing
- quantitative versus qualitative evidence
- updating and belief revision
- incentives and conflicting objectives
- identity, ownership and escalation of commitment
- hierarchy, power and psychological safety
- group discussion and information aggregation
- dissent, disagreement and consensus
- leadership behaviour
- organisational politics
- decision architecture and choice structure
- time pressure
- reversibility and commitment
- outcome bias, hindsight and luck
- ethical considerations
- organisational culture

First: interrogate the research question

Before proposing a case, ask:

1. What phenomenon are we actually trying to explain?
2. What would count as a good decision?
3. How should decision quality be distinguished from outcome quality?
4. Which parts of decision-making are descriptive questions and which are normative?
5. What competing theories could explain the same observed decision?
6. Which mechanisms are genuinely empirically supported?
7. Which are popular narratives with weaker evidence?
8. Which relationships are causal and which are merely correlational?
9. Under what boundary conditions do the effects change or disappear?
10. What important variables are currently missing from the framework?

Second: conduct a critical research review

Review the relevant literature in:

- cognitive psychology
- behavioural economics
- judgement and decision-making
- metacognition
- organisational behaviour
- organisational psychology
- group decision-making
- management science
- decision science
- behavioural operations
- risk and uncertainty
- leadership and hierarchy
- psychological safety
- debiasing
- choice architecture
- escalation of commitment
- information processing

Prioritise recent evidence, particularly systematic reviews, meta-analyses, preregistered studies, large experiments, field studies and strong theoretical syntheses, while retaining foundational research where necessary.

For every important proposed mechanism, identify:

- what the evidence actually shows
- competing explanations
- effect size or practical significance where available
- boundary conditions
- methodological weaknesses
- replication evidence
- whether the finding generalises to organisations
- whether the mechanism can realistically be manipulated in a case or simulation

Do not assume that a well-known bias is automatically important in real organisational decisions.

Third: generate competing hypotheses

Develop a broad hypothesis set, but then reduce it to the smallest number of mechanisms that can explain the largest amount of interesting behaviour.

For each hypothesis specify:

- mechanism
- predicted behaviour
- assumptions
- independent variable
- dependent variable
- plausible alternative explanation
- strongest evidence
- weakest point in the evidence
- confidence in the hypothesis
- how it could be tested experimentally

Pay particular attention to hypotheses concerning:

- anchoring
- information weighting
- quantification fixation
- updating
- shared versus unique information
- first-speaker effects
- dissent
- confidence
- hierarchy
- incentives
- ownership
- escalation of commitment
- information sequencing
- decision architecture
- time pressure
- uncertainty
- reversibility
- outcome bias
- luck

Fourth: determine what the case must contain

Only after identifying the strongest mechanisms should you design the organisational scenario.

The case should contain genuine ambiguity rather than a hidden "correct" answer.

Different participants should be able to reach different conclusions for legitimate reasons.

Create:

- incomplete but sufficient information
- conflicting evidence
- quantitative and qualitative information
- uncertainty that cannot be completely resolved
- stakeholders with legitimate but conflicting objectives
- meaningful trade-offs
- information that is shared by everyone
- information that is privately held by particular participants
- consequences for different stakeholders
- time pressure
- some degree of hierarchy or status
- reputational or identity stakes
- a meaningful but imperfect decision deadline
- the possibility of revising the decision
- an outcome that contains an element of randomness

Avoid making one option obviously rational and the others obviously irrational.

Fifth: design the case as an experiment

Explore whether the same underlying organisational problem can be presented under different decision architectures.

For example:

**Condition A — Individual judgement**

Participants decide independently before discussion.

**Condition B — Unstructured group discussion**

Participants discuss freely and reach a collective decision.

**Condition C — Structured deliberation**

Participants must state assumptions, probabilities, uncertainties and dissenting arguments.

**Condition D — Information sequencing**

Different groups receive the same information in different orders.

**Condition E — Hierarchy**

The perceived preferences or status of senior decision-makers are manipulated.

**Condition F — Incentives**

Participants have different legitimate objectives or incentives.

**Condition G — Revision**

New evidence arrives after the initial decision.

**Condition H — Outcome**

The eventual organisational outcome is partially stochastic.

Determine which of these manipulations are theoretically justified and experimentally tractable rather than including them simply because they are interesting.

Sixth: identify what should actually be measured

Do not measure only whether participants selected the "right" option.

Consider measuring:

- initial choice
- confidence
- probability estimates
- assumptions
- information recalled
- information weighted
- uncertainty acknowledged
- willingness to revise
- magnitude of belief updating
- dissent expressed
- dissent suppressed
- unique information discussed
- time to decision
- consensus
- confidence after consensus
- perceived influence of other participants
- perceived hierarchy
- perceived psychological safety
- perceived accountability
- perceived fairness
- decision-process quality
- final outcome
- retrospective evaluation of the decision

Distinguish carefully between:

**decision quality → process quality → outcome quality → perceived quality.**

These should not be treated as interchangeable.

Seventh: ask what would falsify the emerging theory

For each proposed mechanism ask:

- What result would contradict it?
- What result would suggest the mechanism is weaker than expected?
- What result would suggest an alternative mechanism explains the behaviour?
- What result would show that the intervention itself caused the effect?
- What result would demonstrate that a commonly assumed "bias" is actually adaptive in this environment?

Actively search for evidence against the preferred interpretation.

Eighth: identify the most interesting unresolved question

After reviewing the evidence, determine what the case could uniquely reveal.

In particular investigate whether:

**good organisational decision-making is primarily a property of individual decision-makers, or whether it emerges from the interaction between individuals and the decision environment.**

Also investigate whether the most important observable behaviour is not the initial decision itself but **how people respond when the evidence changes**.

Final output

Produce:

1. A refined theoretical model of organisational decision-making under uncertainty.
2. The strongest empirically supported mechanisms.
3. The weakest or most speculative mechanisms.
4. The key competing explanations.
5. The smallest set of hypotheses worth testing.
6. A proposed organisational case capable of testing them.
7. The experimental conditions and manipulations.
8. The measures and outcome variables.
9. Predictions for each condition.
10. Threats to validity.
11. What results would support, weaken or falsify each hypothesis.
12. The most surprising findings or tensions in the literature.
13. The most important questions that the current research cannot answer.

The objective is not to create a case that demonstrates that people are irrational.

The objective is to create a case that allows us to investigate:

**How does organisational judgement actually emerge when imperfect humans interact with imperfect information, uncertainty, incentives, social dynamics and institutional structures?**

But there is an even better set of questions

If I could go back and interrogate the project **before designing anything**, these are the questions I'd most want answered:

1. What are we actually trying to explain?

Not *“What decision will they make?”*

But:

> **Why do otherwise reasonable people make different decisions when given the same organisational problem?**
> 

That immediately opens up cognition, information, incentives, power, identity and context.

2. What makes a decision "good"?

This is surprisingly fundamental.

Is it:

- choosing the objectively best action?
- making the best decision given the information available at the time?
- having a defensible process?
- being appropriately calibrated about uncertainty?
- producing a good outcome?
- being able to explain and revise the decision?

Those are different constructs.

3. Where is the uncertainty?

I'd ask this *before* choosing the organisation.

Is the uncertainty:

- **aleatory** — the future genuinely contains randomness?
- **epistemic** — we don't know something that could theoretically be known?
- **model uncertainty** — competing models produce different predictions?
- **strategic uncertainty** — other actors respond to us?
- **conceptual uncertainty** — we aren't even sure what variables matter?

This could become one of the most interesting features of the entire simulation.

4. Why would intelligent people disagree?

This may be the most important design question.

If everyone has the same incentives, information and objectives, disagreement risks becoming a story about who is "biased."

Instead:

> **Can two intelligent people reasonably interpret the same evidence differently because they are optimising different legitimate objectives?**
> 

Now disagreement becomes informative.

5. Who knows what?

I'd map the information structure explicitly.

Some information should be:

- common knowledge
- privately held
- ambiguous
- unreliable
- quantified
- qualitative
- emotionally salient
- strategically important
- revealed only later

That gives you the possibility of studying **information aggregation**, rather than merely asking people to make choices.

6. What changes when people interact?

This is where the case could become genuinely interesting.

Does discussion:

- reveal information?
- suppress information?
- amplify confidence?
- correct errors?
- create new errors?
- produce conformity?
- expose assumptions?
- allow minority information to surface?
- cause people to defend positions they previously held weakly?

In other words:

> **Does the group become more intelligent than its members, or does interaction change the error structure?**
> 

7. What happens when the evidence changes?

I would make this central.

Don't just observe:

**Decision A → outcome.**

Observe:

**Initial belief → new evidence → revision → second decision → outcome.**

That gives you a much richer object of study.

Someone making an imperfect initial decision but updating appropriately may demonstrate better decision-making than someone who happened to make the correct initial choice.

8. What role does the decision process itself play?

This is the question I think became increasingly important during our discussion.

Suppose exactly the same people receive exactly the same information.

But:

- one group discusses immediately;
- another makes independent judgements first;
- another must identify assumptions;
- another must nominate a dissenting view;
- another sees information sequentially.

If their decisions differ, you've learned something profound:

> **The decision isn't simply produced by the decision-makers. It is partly produced by the architecture surrounding them.**
> 

9. What happens when the outcome is random?

This question is essential if you want to study organisational decision-making rather than just decision outcomes.

Imagine two groups make different decisions.

One happens to succeed.

The other happens to fail.

If observers subsequently rate the successful group's process as better—even though they had identical information quality at the time—you've captured **outcome bias** and the role of luck.

That gives you an opportunity to separate:

**“Was this a good decision?”**

from

**“Did this decision work?”**

10. What would surprise us?

I'd explicitly ask this before running the study.

For example:

- What if more information makes decisions worse?
- What if experts are less willing to revise?
- What if dissent improves accuracy but reduces perceived team quality?
- What if consensus increases confidence without increasing accuracy?
- What if the most influential person isn't the most knowledgeable?
- What if structured decision-making improves process but makes people slower?
- What if a "bias" is actually adaptive under particular conditions?
- What if the biggest effect comes not from individual psychology but from information architecture?

Those are much more interesting findings than simply demonstrating another cognitive bias.

And the biggest change I'd make

I would **not start with the 40 hypotheses**.

Those were useful as a map, but they're too numerous to be the conceptual starting point.

I'd start with perhaps **four layers**:

**Individual**

> How do people interpret, weight and update information?
> 

**Interaction**

> What happens when those people exchange information?
> 

**Organisation**

> How do incentives, hierarchy, identity and accountability alter that interaction?
> 

**Architecture**

> How does the structure of the decision process itself alter what happens?
> 

Then ask:

> **Which layer explains the greatest amount of variation in decision quality, and how do the layers interact?**
> 

That gives the project a much stronger intellectual spine.

And it leads to what I now think is the deepest version of the original question:

> **When an organisation makes a decision, how much of that decision comes from the people in the room, how much comes from the information they receive, how much comes from the relationships between them, and how much comes from the structure of the decision itself?**
> 

That is a much richer research programme than simply building a case designed to expose people to cognitive biases.
